# The read-back list

Go through this after you have a draft, not while you write. Each mark has an
example from the old text and what to do about it.

One mark alone is nothing. People write lists of three and the odd semicolon
too. Look for clusters. When a line or a paragraph trips two or three, do not
edit the marks out. Go back to the method: who is reading, what do they need,
say it out loud, write that down.

Avoiding these is not the goal. The goal is a line a person would say. A text
can dodge every mark here and still sound made by a machine, because it dodged
them. That is how the second voice below came about.

## What a person's rules have that ours lacked

Counted over 4,227 words of help in six games, against nine manuals written by
people:

|                     | Ours, per 1,000 words | Manuals |
| ------------------- | --------------------: | ------: |
| if                  |                     0 |     8.1 |
| will                |                     0 |    11.0 |
| you can             |                   0.2 |     5.7 |
| you, your           |                  10.4 |    25.6 |
| a count spelled out |                  11.1 |     6.8 |

So the first check is for what is missing. In a paragraph of rules, look for
"you", for "if" or "when", and for "can". If none is there, the paragraph is
describing a mechanism, not talking to a player.

- Old: "A ball that falls past the paddle costs a life."
- Better: "If the ball gets past your paddle, you lose a life."

## The newer voice

These are the habits of the text written here from October 2026 on.

**Three things in a row, for the sound of it.** "Eat, grow and keep clear of
your own tail." "Fly, drift and blast every rock." When three things really
happen in order, list them. When you reached for a third to finish the rhythm,
cut to the one that matters.

**A thing given a person's verb.** "Iron holds its hits." "Stars open nothing."
"A best made under earlier rules says so." "Bricks pay double." Things in a game
break, bounce, fall, move, score. Say what happens to the player or what the
player sees. "Bricks score double."

**A semicolon or a colon joining two thoughts.** "Point; the guide follows."
"Never breaks: round, it kicks every fuzzball away." Write two sentences. A
colon after a real label is fine: "Stock: the cards left over."

**"As" for when, and for like.** "As it breaks, zaps every brick in its row."
"Hits the ship as a rock does." Use "when" for time and "like" for likeness, or
turn the sentence around: "Zaps every brick in its row when it breaks."

**A thing's possessive.** "The floor's row", "the level's boon", "the bar's
Sound button", "static's multiplier". Say "the bottom row", "the boon for this
level", "the Sound button".

**The word that is not the ordinary word.** "Go on" for resume. "Call a charge
off" for cancel. "Put away" for close. "By itself" for automatically. "Turns"
for bounces. "The bed" for the music. Use the word a player would use. The
[word list](words.md) has the pairs.

**A key named by a finger.** "The key above the right little finger's home key."
That is P.

**Words squeezed out.** "Every brick a level needs", "a digit the solution
doesn't hold", "the squares it sees", "those still waiting over the ceiling
among them". Each is correct and each makes the reader stop. Put the small words
back, or say it a longer, plainer way: "the same row, column and box".

**It, one, those, the last.** "The level is lost once one it needs stands in the
floor's row." Repeat the noun. "If a brick reaches the floor, you lose the
level."

**Help as a specification.** Every edge case and exact number, in one long
sentence with five commas. A player learns most of it by playing. Keep what they
need to start.

**Explaining the interface.** "On a card's button, the keys are the button's:
Space and Enter press it." If a control needs a sentence to explain how it
works, look at the control.

**A private name for part of the screen.** The bed, the bench, the deck, the
Ready card, the bar. The player was never told these. Name the button, or say
where it is.

**"Whichever", "whatever", "alone", "of its own".** "The keys aim and charge
whichever way is chosen." "The deck has Dash alone." Say the plain thing: "The
keys work in both modes." "Only the Dash button shows."

**One level tone for everything.** A rule, an error, a tooltip and a win all in
the same even voice. At a win, you can sound glad.

## The older voice

This is the one most people recognize. It was in Solitaire.

**Capitals for stress.** "Built UP in the SAME SUIT." Say it plainly and it
needs no stress: "Build up by suit."

**A title on every rule.** "Supermove Capacity Formula:", "Stock Recycle:",
"Kings Sink:". A label names a pile on the table. A sentence does not need a
name.

**Em dashes.** "Always 104 cards — fewer suits means more copies of each, and a
far gentler game." Two sentences, or a comma.

**Stock words.** initially, respectively, automatically, successfully, highly,
simply, configurable, "in order to", "allows you to", "(e.g., …)". Also praise
with nothing behind it: "a highly strategic solitaire game".

**The passive, with nobody in it.** "Cards can be placed on any tableau card of
next higher rank." Put the player back: "You can put a card on any card one rank
higher."

**A form filled in.** The same seven headings for every game, whether or not it
has anything to say under them. Use a section only where there is something to
put in it.

**"Congratulations! You have successfully…"** "You won!"

## The usual lists

You know the well-worn ones: delve, seamless, elevate, "not just X but Y",
"whether you're a beginner or a pro", "dive in", a closing line that sums up
what was just said. None of them belongs here either.
