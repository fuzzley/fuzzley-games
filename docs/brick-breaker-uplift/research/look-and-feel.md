# Look and feel: how paddle brick breakers draw, move and sound

Read 2026-11-10 from wikis, reviews, art write-ups, interviews, talk summaries
and design essays, through a fetch tool that summarises, so wording is
paraphrased. No game source code was read and no asset was copied. "(inferred)"
marks my own reading, "(listing)" marks a fact taken only from a search result
summary of a page I could not open, and "(weak)" marks a page that read as
machine-written or held a plain error. The talks themselves were not watched.

## 1. How notable paddle breakers look

- **Arkanoid (1986), neon after Tron.** The designer was a Tron fan and the
  look is futuristic neon. Blocks were first all one colour and colour came
  later; enemies and capsules were hand-drawn from 3D models, then turned into
  sprites, which would explain their rounded, lit look (inferred).
  [Wikipedia Arkanoid]
- **Arkanoid, material says how tough.** Coloured bricks take one hit, silver
  ones several, gold ones never break: hue means "ordinary", metal means
  "durable". Silver needs 2 hits in rounds 1-8, rising to 5 by rounds 25-32
  (listing). [Pixelated Arcade] [Arcade History]
- **Arkanoid, seven capsules told apart by colour.** Orange slows, green
  catches, blue widens, aqua splits the ball in three, red gives lasers, pink
  opens an exit on the right wall, grey is a life; each also carries a letter
  (listing) and is said to pulse so the eye finds it (weak).
  [Pixelated Arcade] [Arcade History] [Retro Replay Arkanoid]
- **Arkanoid, the cabinet.** It shipped only as a conversion kit (board,
  marquee, side art, monitor bezel, spinner), so cabinets differ. The spinner
  gave variable paddle speed that home joysticks lacked. [Pixelated Arcade]
- **Arkanoid: Eternal Battle, two skins and a busy backdrop.** Retro mode
  shows the 1986 game inside an arcade-cabinet frame with arcade ambience
  (listing); Neo mode adds neon colour, which one critic still calls dated.
  In battle mode rivals' boards flicker behind your own, and a critic finds
  that distracting and his ball hard to follow.
  [PSU] [NoobFeed] [WTMG Eternal] [GameSpew Eternal]
- **Breakout (1976), colour as bands.** A black-and-white screen under tinted
  cellophane strips: rows in pairs of yellow, green, orange, red from the
  bottom, worth 1, 3, 5 and 7. Colour is the score table. [Wikipedia Breakout]
- **Breakout: Recharged, faux vector in blue and purple.** Clean glowing
  lines, widened from 4:3. Multi-hit bricks are drawn as little brick walls,
  glowing bricks explode, and power-up bricks carry an up arrow. Critics say
  the vector style does not suit the game and looks bland or washed out.
  [Tech-Gaming] [GameSpew Recharged] [WTMG Recharged]
- **Breakout Beyond (2025), a light show on black.** Sideways play on a mostly
  black field with a CRT-like filter, a very thin paddle of light, and a new
  colour scheme per level. The ball starts slow, then glows, speeds up and
  leaves afterimages as a rally builds. [NWR Beyond] [GameLuster] [TheXboxHub]
- **Shatter (2009), clear shapes first.** Compared to Geometry Wars: crisp,
  minimal, mechanical, with bright bricks over an abstract backdrop. The bat
  sits at the bottom, on the left wall, or inside a circular arena, and levels
  flip into the next one. [Destructoid Shatter] [Higher Plain] [PS Lifestyle]
- **Shatter, bricks have behaviour, not just colour.** Some stay put, some
  break loose and drift at the bat; others fire like rockets, explode, blow
  the ball away or cluster like cells. Shards fly to the bat and fill a power
  bar. [Destructoid Shatter] [Wikipedia Shatter] [LifeisXbox]
- **Wizorb, a 16-bit RPG in miniature.** Vibrant pixel art, rich animation
  and careful menus; the wizard turns into a wand (paddle) and an orb (ball).
  [Destructoid Wizorb] [Pocket Gamer Wizorb] [Gemsbok]
- **Strikey Sisters, plain pixels on purpose.** A girl swings a bat or sword
  in place of a paddle. One critic praises the modest modern-pixel look as a
  relief from the shiny metallic clones of the late 1990s.
  [Buried Treasure] [Indie Gamer Chick]
- **BreakQuest, every level its own picture.** 100 levels, each with its own
  look and sound; bricks of every shape hang from threads and swing when hit.
  Themes include a billiard table, a bowling alley and Space Invaders.
  [Gamezebo BreakQuest] [Fun-Motion] [Wikipedia BreakQuest]
- **Ricochet Lost Worlds, worlds with living backdrops (weak).** Underwater,
  volcano and alien worlds each have a palette and an animated backdrop
  (shimmering water, bubbling lava, drifting dust); bricks spin, shake or sway
  in columns like a shoal. [Retro Replay Lost Worlds]
- **Ricochet Infinity, the paddle is a ship you earn.** Rings hidden in levels
  unlock new ships and ball designs; environments have names (Alien Vista, Pod
  People, Canvas). Some levels act out a scene, such as a brick prisoner freed
  and chased by a brick police car.
  [JayIsGames] [Ricochet wiki] [Wikipedia Ricochet Infinity]
- **DX-Ball 2, texture and board-sets.** High-colour textured bricks and
  backdrops, additive-blend explosions, a ray-traced ball, and a theme per
  board-set. The series colours its capsules blue for good, red for bad and
  grey for "it depends": 10, 5 and 5 in the first DX-Ball (listing).
  [Wikipedia DX-Ball 2] [DX-Ball listing]
- **Block Breaker Deluxe, 1980s neon nightlife.** Crisp and clean, but one
  critic warns there is so much neon it tires the eyes; another finds the
  cartoon art dated. A new backdrop unlocks every ten stages. [NL BBD] [iLounge]
- **Alphabounce and Twin Breaker, story over a plain look.** Alphabounce is
  said to look like one more generic clone, carried by 40 brick types and a
  C64-flavoured score. Twin Breaker's NES-and-arcade look is called eclectic
  and its story praised for staying out of the way (listing).
  [NL Alphabounce] [Xbox Tavern]
- **Drawkanoid, effects built for two speeds.** Particles do the work, with
  only a few bloom passes. A break must read at full speed, while debris and
  small details are made to show during the slow-motion. [80.lv Drawkanoid]
- **Mobile, themes as wrappers.** Brick Breaker Hero gives the paddle to a
  hero with a shield and themes each kingdom; Breakforcist's bricks are
  waffles and eggs haunted by ghosts; Brick Dungeon sells four skins (jelly,
  cyberpunk, stone, retro CRT). Anodia is mostly muted, and its few colourful
  levels are said to bring it to life (listing). Nothing found on Many Bricks
  Breaker or Brick Breaker Star beyond store text. [Brick Breaker Hero listing]
  [Pocket Gamer Breakforcist] [Brick Dungeon listing] [TouchArcade Anodia]

## 2. Game feel techniques

- **Juice it or lose it, the layers in order.** Colour; tweening of position,
  rotation and scale with easing; a random delay per object; paddle stretch by
  speed; ball rotation and stretch; wobble; colour change and block scale on
  impact; sounds for wall, block and paddle; music; impact particles; a block
  hit animation; shatter; ball trail; screen shake; big eyes, a blink and a
  smile; screen colour; multiple balls. [Bayreuth handout]
- **Juice it or lose it, the argument.** Juice is "maximum output for minimum
  input": a tiny action answered generously. Crude art is fine, the speakers
  say, but a lack of response feels wrong. Tweening plus a random delay per
  brick is what makes a wall arrive, not appear (inferred); no source gave
  durations. [GD juicy enough]
- **The Art of Screenshake, the parts that carry over.** Among its 30 tweaks:
  an impact effect, a hit animation, knockback, things that stay (casings,
  smoke), camera lerp, shake, a short pause on hit, more bass in the sounds, a
  camera kick, random explosions, slow motion. [Bayreuth handout]
- **Shake has a direction.** Vlambeer moves the screen against the line of
  fire. Drawkanoid has four kinds of shake that follow the hit, and a hit
  flash that throws particles along the ball's path. A survey argues an eased
  move in a meaningful direction says more than random jitter.
  [GD Vlambeer] [80.lv Drawkanoid] [Game feel survey]
- **Shake, how long.** One guide gives 0.15-0.25 s for a hit and about 0.3 s
  for an explosion: fast and small, not slow and large, always decaying, with
  little or no rotation. Past about 1.5 s it stops reading as an impact.
  [StraySpark]
- **Hit stop, a few frames, and rarely.** A hit stop pauses the objects
  involved; a freeze frame pauses everything. Sakurai scales it with the
  blow, caps it, and avoids it where hits come thick and fast because it
  slows play, which is a breaker's case (inferred). Tutorial lore says 2-3
  frames for light hits, 4-6 for heavy, and over 20 looks like a bug (listing).
  [Game feel survey] [Sakurai hit stop] [Hit-stop listings]
- **Shake the thing, not the camera.** Sakurai shakes the struck object more,
  starts large and shrinks, and keeps the hit area still so collisions stay
  honest. For us: wobble the brick and its neighbours, never the collision
  box (inferred). [Sakurai hit stop] [Nintendo Wire]
- **Squash and stretch, no numbers anywhere.** Sources only say to keep volume
  and to match the amount to the material (listing). Swink adds that a squishy
  thing needs just a deformation and a squelch, not a simulation.
  [Swink feature]
- **Easing rules of thumb.** Never move linearly; ease out for things that
  arrive; keep elastic easing for lively games. Trails act like smears and can
  make a move feel slower. [GD squeezing juice]
- **Slow motion as help, not just show.** Drawkanoid slows time when the ball
  enters the lower half, timed to the moment the player can act. Breakout
  Beyond's Focus slows play at a cost in score; one critic leaned on it for
  most of the game. [Entertainium] [80.lv Drawkanoid] [TheXboxHub]
- **The finale, built by piling on.** Peggle's Extreme Fever began as a joke:
  the word "Fever" and Beethoven's Ninth. Flames, rainbows and slow motion
  were added one by one, and it stayed because it felt so gratifying.
  [Gamezebo Peggle]
- **Score fly-ups.** In Plants vs. Zombies a coin flashes and spins on its way
  to the total; the small payoff is the point. Big celebration for a minor act
  feels false. [GD juicy term]
- **Faces and reactions.** The talk's last layers are eyes, a blink and a
  smile on the paddle. PopCap's Bookworm is called juicy because it mirrors
  the player's mood; Kirby does a victory jig on a cleared screen.
  [Bayreuth handout] [GD juicy term] [NL Kirby]
- **How much is too much.** A study of four versions of one game (N = 3018,
  listing) found both no juice and extreme juice worse than medium or high for
  play time, enjoyment and performance. Critics add that abundant feedback
  hides what matters to the rules, and that polish can cost immersion.
  [Game feel survey] [Kao study] [GD don't juice]
- **Juice the frequent act.** Dressing up rare events does little; the
  everyday action deserves the care. In a breaker that is the paddle hit and
  the brick break (inferred). [Brad Woods]

## 3. Readability

- **An order of attention for breakers.** Paddle and ball first, then level
  goals, threats and bad capsules, good capsules, score pop-ups, text
  exclamations, and ordinary blocks last. Anything moving, pulsing, glowing or
  loud climbs that order, so glow is a budget for the ball and the specials
  (inferred). [Breaking Down Breakout]
- **Keeping the ball visible.** Suggested aids are a larger ball, a colour
  nothing else uses, and a comet tail; the ball must stay readable at top
  speed. LEGO Bricktopia disturbs the backdrop texture under the ball.
  [Breaking Down Breakout]
- **Plan the values, then test in grey.** Classic breakers used black for
  contrast. With a backdrop, give the things to track the brightest,
  highest-contrast values, and check that a desaturated screenshot still reads
  at once. [Breaking Down Breakout] [GD visual confusion]
- **Two drawing styles.** Ronimo draws play objects with outlines and firm
  shading, and backdrops painterly, desaturated and slightly blurred, brighter
  near the play layer. Leftover clutter (corpses) is desaturated too.
  [Ronimo post]
- **Where breakers lose the ball.** Enemy shots the size of the ball (Strikey
  Sisters); explosion effects (Block Breaker Deluxe); afterimages and glow
  (Breakout Beyond); several balls at once (Shatter); sheer debris and items
  (BreakQuest, Ricochet Infinity).
  [Indie Gamer Chick] [NL BBD] [GameLuster] [PS Lifestyle] [Gamezebo BreakQuest]
- **Damage states found.** Shade (Alleyway: darker is worth more), material
  (Arkanoid's silver and gold), and a drawn texture (Recharged's little wall).
  Colour may decorate or may mean hit count, but must not do both. No write-up
  compared cracks, pips and numbers.
  [Wikipedia Alleyway] [Tech-Gaming] [Breaking Down Breakout]
- **Good versus bad capsules.** Bad ones are usually obvious, but in a crowd
  players cannot tell them apart (BreakQuest), and an itch player asks for a
  letter or own colour per power-up (listing).
  [Breaking Down Breakout] [Gamezebo BreakQuest] [itch comments]
- **Colour-blind practice.** Carry nothing essential by colour alone. Two Dots
  overlays a symbol per colour; ReCore pairs colour with an arrow; Peggle's
  patent describes hash marks on pegs (listing).
  [GAG basic] [Access pattern] [Peggle patent]
- **Flicker and motion.** Avoid flicker and repeating patterns. Xbox's guide
  asks for a way to stop content that blinks or moves behind text, and to
  avoid camera shake or let it be turned off; HyperDot has a switch for its
  animated backdrop. [GAG basic] [XAG 117]

## 4. Audio

- **Alleyway, pitch by surface.** Walls give the lowest note and black bricks
  the highest, so the ear knows what was hit. Music plays only in bonus
  stages. [Wikipedia Alleyway]
- **BreakQuest, every object its own tone.** Most things sound different when
  struck, which the writer calls synesthetic; each level also has its own
  audio theme. [Fun-Motion]
- **Peggle 2, hits are the melody.** Each peg hit steps up a diatonic scale
  chosen to fit the harmony of the phrase playing, and the scale changes with
  the music even mid-shot. The music calms when the player idles and keeps
  its deepest bass for the finale. [Peggle 2 audio]
- **Rising pitch is cheap variety (listing).** Peggle Blast covers all its hit
  notes with five samples, and its audio team calls rising pitch on one sound
  a win for variety and excitement at no extra asset. [Peggle Blast listing]
- **Shatter, the score is half the game.** Over 90 minutes of music in tracks
  of three to nine minutes. A first score triggered by game events was
  scrapped after seven months: it worked but had no energy. What stayed live
  is small: the audio phases out on a death and a filter follows play.
  [GD Shatter IGF] [Ongaku] [Designing Sound]
- **Recharged, music that stays out of the way.** McDuffee aims for players
  who are pumped but not distracted, with strong leads and punchy mixes.
  Critics call it the best part, but the default volume is far too loud and
  the effects are thin. [Magnetic McDuffee] [WTMG Recharged]
- **Breakout Beyond, hits make a beat.** The paddle hit sounds like a tennis
  ball; brick hits fall into a rhythm as a combo builds; the music ramps, a
  robot voice reads out "BREAK OUT", and a soft melody follows the clear.
  Strikey Sisters shouts its pickups in a cheesy arcade voice.
  [NWR Beyond] [Seafoam] [GameLuster] [Buried Treasure]
- **Mixing.** Play sounds should be the loudest and fullest in the mix, with
  quiet ambient loops under them; give separate volumes for effects and music.
  [GD squeezing juice] [GAG basic]
- **Panning and haptics, little found.** No prose source on panning by ball
  position. One store page lists three tiers of haptics, from a tick per hit
  to a thud per blast. On the web, vibration is missing from some major
  browsers and silently does nothing there.
  [Brick Dungeon listing] [MDN Vibration]

## 5. Character and theme

- **Arkanoid and Breakout, a story in a few lines.** The Vaus is an escape
  craft from a destroyed mothership, fighting the entity DOH; that alone turns
  a bat into a ship. Breakout's cabinet art shows a prisoner knocking down his
  cell wall. [Wikipedia Arkanoid] [Wikipedia Breakout]
- **Alleyway, a mascot on the box is not a theme (inferred).** Mario is on the
  cover of the Western release, and bonus stages draw Mario, Blooper and
  Bowser in bricks, yet critics still call it bland. [Wikipedia Alleyway]
- **Kirby's Block Ball, the mascot is the ball.** HAL felt an early build was
  not Kirby and spent six months reworking it, with close direction on how he
  moves. He unfurls on a timed hit, dances on a clear, and each of 11 worlds
  ends with a boss from his other games. [Wikipedia Kirby] [NL Kirby]
- **Wizorb, a village between levels.** Coins rebuild a ruined town whose
  people can be talked to. A critic finds it charming but beside the point,
  as little time is spent there. [Destructoid Wizorb]
- **Shatter, story told by music.** The bat, BAT1138, escapes a factory; the
  tracks and a robot voice that grows bolder carry it. Bosses are praised for
  personality. [GD Shatter IGF] [Ongaku] [PS Lifestyle]
- **Strikey Sisters and Ricochet, cast and collection.** Two sisters, monster
  cards to collect, and voiced banter; Ricochet's ships and balls are both
  identity and reward. [Indie Gamer Chick] [JayIsGames]
- **World theming that works.** A palette, a backdrop and a brick behaviour
  per world (Lost Worlds, weak), a theme per board-set (DX-Ball 2), a new
  element per world (Kirby). [Retro Replay Lost Worlds] [Wikipedia DX-Ball 2]
- **Wrappers that jar.** Block Breaker Deluxe's nightlife story sits oddly
  with its fantasy bosses (listing); BreakQuest's cutscenes are easy to
  ignore. [GameZone] [iLounge] [Gamezebo BreakQuest]

## 6. HUD and in-field information

- **Alleyway and Kirby, small icons with a job.** A fire flower, a mushroom
  and a star appear under Alleyway's score at 10,000, 20,000 and 30,000
  points. Kirby's stages open with him on the paddle and an arrow sweeping the
  launch angles. [Wikipedia Alleyway] [NL Kirby]
- **Breakout Beyond, a clock and slots.** A three-minute timer runs in the
  level, and a preview shows which power-up sits in which slot. [GameLuster]
- **Shatter, one bar fed by the field.** Shards fly to the bat and fill a
  power bar; bosses have a health bar that drops only when a weak spot is hit
  (listing). [Wikipedia Shatter] [LifeisXbox] [Glitchwave]
- **Wizorb and BreakQuest, the field explains itself.** Wizorb's shop is a
  doorway at the top of the field that the orb enters; BreakQuest states a
  level's objective in a small tip panel.
  [Pocket Gamer Wizorb] [Breaking Down Breakout]
- **Clutter complaints.** Ricochet Infinity's on-screen mascot annoys players
  and can be switched off; players of small breakers report boss bars too
  faint to notice (listing). Text over a moving field wants an opaque backing.
  [JayIsGames] [itch comments] [XAG 117]
- **Not found.** No review discusses how a paddle breaker shows the time left
  on a power-up (a ring, a bar on the paddle, a flash before expiry). One
  player asks for any visible cue for a 10-second effect (listing).
  [itch comments]

## What stands out for our game

- **1. A backdrop per level set, held back in value.** Nothing changes an
  empty panel more, and world theming is what marks out Ricochet and DX-Ball 2. Risk: it fights the bricks, as in Eternal Battle; keep it dim,
  desaturated, slow, and still under reduced motion.
- **2. Bricks that arrive and a wall that reacts.** Staggered tween-in, then a
  flash, a scale pop and a wobble in the neighbours on each hit: the cheapest
  layers in the talk. Risk: an entry that delays a retry; keep it under a
  second.
- **3. Let the mascot ball act.** Stretch along travel, squash and wobble on a
  bounce, wide eyes at speed, a jig on a clear. Kirby shows a mascot ball
  earns months of care on movement. Risk: the drawn shape leaving the hit
  circle; deform the picture only.
- **4. Give the paddle a body.** Stretch with speed, a dip on impact with its
  own sound, perhaps a face that watches the ball. Risk: soft edges on the
  one object whose edges decide the bounce.
- **5. A pitch ladder in key and a voice per surface.** Step streak hits up a
  scale, give wall, paddle and brick distinct timbres, and tune the clear
  stinger to match. Risk: a ladder that tops out shrill; cap it.
- **6. A quiet music bed that follows play.** Even one loop per level set,
  thinned when idle and filtered on a lost ball, would end the silence. Risk:
  repetition and loudness (Recharged's fault); separate volume, low default.
- **7. A finale for the last brick.** A brief slow-down, a burst and the
  stinger, in Peggle's spirit; Shatter's makers named the last brick as a
  genre problem. Risk: tiresome every level; keep it near a second.
- **8. One visual vocabulary for special bricks.** Glow or pulse only for
  explosive ones, a drawn material for unbreakable ones, an icon for ones that
  drop capsules. Risk: glow everywhere, so nothing stands out.
- **9. Capsules that read by shape and symbol.** Add a pulse, and a different
  outline for bad ones, since colour alone fails colour-blind players and
  fails everyone in a crowd. Risk: more shapes to learn; keep to two.
- **10. A trail that grows with the streak.** A short comet tail is the
  standard aid for a fast ball and shows the streak without text. Risk:
  Breakout Beyond's afterimages drew clutter complaints; cap the length.
- **11. Directed shake, tiny, and hit stop only for big events.** Move the
  field along the ball's path for 0.15-0.25 s on a blast; freeze a few frames
  only for a boss hit or the last brick. Risk: nausea; add an amount slider.
- **12. Debris that fades back.** Shards should desaturate and dim as they
  fall so they never compete with the ball. Cost and risk are both small.
- **13. Lives and timers drawn in the field.** Spare mascots as life icons,
  and a ring or bar on the paddle for a timed power-up. No source showed how
  best to do it, so it needs our own mock-ups. Risk: a cluttered paddle.
- **14. Keep the dose at medium to high, never extreme.** The one large study
  found both ends worse. Test the busiest moment in greyscale with three
  balls: the ball should still be the first thing seen.
