# Machine-writing tells: research notes

Research for the text style guide. The question: what makes prose read as written by an AI model, and which of those signs can turn up in short product text (labels, tooltips, how-to-play text, blurbs, release notes, help pages).

Researched on 2026-11-16.

## 1. Method and conventions

- Pages were downloaded with `curl` and read as plain text, because the WebFetch summariser paraphrases and refused to reproduce the Wikipedia page. Wikipedia was read from its raw wikitext, all 1,480 lines, plus its Style subpage.
- Text in "double quotes" with a source named is copied from the page as fetched in this session. Wikipedia examples have their wiki markup (highlights, links, italics) removed and nothing else changed. Quotation marks nested inside a quote are written as single marks, and curly marks are sometimes written straight. `[...]` or `[…]` inside a quote is the source's own elision. A quote may be part of a longer sentence. Where I left out words in the middle, I closed the quotation marks and reopened them.
- Examples marked (mine) are invented illustrations, not quotes. They are there only to show how a tell would look in a label or a blurb.
- "Search summary only" means the page itself was not fetched, so nothing from it is quoted.
- Section 5's "short text" column is my judgement unless a source is named. Most sources studied essays, articles and chat replies. Graphite says so about its own work: "Our documents are web articles. Tells may differ in other contexts, such as conversations with AI models."
- Model names (GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, Gemini 4 Argon) are given as the sources give them.

## 2. Findings in brief

1. Word lists age fast. Wikipedia tracks four vocabulary eras since 2023, and Graphite measured that between two consecutive versions of one model family "55% to 72% of tells are unique to one version". The constructions last longer than the words: contrast framing ("not X, but Y", "X rather than Y"), threes, importance flagging, tacked-on "-ing" comment, generic claims.
2. The best-known tells are now over-corrected. Current GPT and Claude models use em dashes far less often than people did before ChatGPT (Graphite: Astra 88% below the human rate, Opus 5.5 more than 99% below). Wikipedia now lists heavy em dash use as a historical sign and notes that near-total reliance on commas and full stops "has become its own sign of AI writing".
3. Second-order tells are well evidenced for one family: compression. Dense half-sentences, borrowed engineering shorthand ("load-bearing", "gated"), coined labels used as if the reader knew them, noun stacks, arrow chains, and metaphor in place of plain statement. Anthropic's own prompting guides describe these in its models and give wording to counter them. Evidence is thinner for archaic phrasing and unusual word order (section 6).
4. A second family is leftover process. Text that describes what was changed, kept or avoided, answers objections nobody raised, or qualifies every claim. Wikipedia's edit-summary section is the closest published study of very short machine-written text, and this is its main finding.
5. Banned-word lists alone fail in a documented way: the model swaps in a neighbour ("Blast radius" banned, "impact surface" appears instead), and "write simply" produced over-explanation of basics. Rules written against one model's habits are wrong for the next.
6. Long-standing plain-language guidance already forbids most of the first-order tells: GOV.UK's "Words to avoid" includes empower, facilitate, foster, key, leverage, robust, streamline, transform and utilise, and says "Avoid using metaphors". But some plain-language habits, applied by rule, are themselves now read as machine writing: bullets with bold lead-ins, a heading on everything, one short sentence after another at the same length.
7. For short text the tells that matter most are: generic lines that fit any product, promotional and evaluative adjectives, contrast and triple shapes in blurbs, "-ing" tails, restating the label in the tooltip, emoji and Title Case as decoration, coined feature names, and customer-service filler in messages.

## 4. Source notes

### 4.1 Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing (WP, WPS)

A field guide kept by WikiProject AI Cleanup, built from real edits. It is the most complete catalogue found and most other catalogues (TF, HZ) borrow from it. It describes itself as "descriptive, not prescriptive" and warns: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder."

Its own summary of the present state: "As of October 2026, the most common signs of newly AI-generated text on Wikipedia (besides the unambiguous indicators above) are Markdown, AI edit summaries, hedging disclaimers about source availability and usage, vague expression of connection or association, and undue emphasis on identification by primary sources."

Its explanation of the cause: models tend to "regress to the mean", so "the LLM tends to omit specific, unusual, nuanced facts (which are statistically rare) and replace them with more generic, positive descriptions (which are statistically common). Thus the highly specific 'inventor of the first train-coupling device' might become 'a revolutionary titan of industry'." And: "The subject becomes simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated."

The taxonomy, section by section, with the page's "words to watch" and one of its examples each.

Two notes from this part that matter for short text. On vocabulary: "One or two of these words appearing in an edit may be coincidental, but an edit (post-2022) introducing lots of them, lots of times, is one of the strongest tells for AI use." And: "a word being overused by AI does not imply that its synonyms are also overused." On threes: "This sign is stronger when it appears in contexts where most people would not bother to include such stylistic flourishes, like edit summaries."

The vocabulary eras, as the page gives them:

- "2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant"
- "Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant"
- "Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing"
- "Mid-2026 and on (GPT-6): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally (with much more period and comma usage and shorter sentences around a strict 8–20 word limit)"

#### Signs of human writing

The page lists constructions that are "more common in Wikipedia articles written by humans than in AI-generated text":

- "Simple is/has phrases, such as there is a, it has a."
- "Words with complex, stiff or euphemistic synonyms, such as wrote (versus authored), moved (versus relocated), used (versus utilized), tried (versus attempted), died (versus passed away)." (The plain word is the human one.)
- "Superlative or definitive statements, such as one of the best, is the only, was the first"
- "Qualifiers and intensifiers, such as very, perhaps, tends to."
- "Isolated wordy constructions such as as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, or the fact that."

#### Ineffective indicators

The page says these do not show machine writing: perfect grammar; a mix of casual and formal register; "bland" or "robotic" prose; "fancy", "academic" or "formal" prose in general ("these are specific words. The correlation does not extend to all formal, academic, or 'fancy'-sounding prose."); transition words in isolation; unsourced content.

- Detectors: "Do not solely rely on artificial intelligence content detection tools (such as GPTZero and Pangram). While they perform better than random chance, these tools have non-trivial error rates."

### 4.3 Studies

Kobak et al. (KB). Word frequencies in "over 15 million biomedical abstracts from 2010–2024 indexed by PubMed". The appearance of LLMs "led to an abrupt increase in the frequency of certain style words"; the "excess word analysis suggests that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs."

- Rare words with the largest rise: "delves (r=28.0), underscores (r=13.8), and showcasing (r=10.7)", r being the ratio of observed to expected frequency.
- Common words with the largest rise: "potential (δ=0.052), findings (δ=0.041), and crucial (δ=0.037)", δ being the gap between the observed and expected share of abstracts that contain the word.
- A set of ten common marker words: "across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within."
- The excess words of 2024 were style words, not topic words: "out of all 379 excess style words in 2024, 66% were verbs and 14% were adjectives".
- Its examples of "LLM-style flowery language" from real 2023 abstracts: "By meticulously delving into the intricate web connecting […] and […], this comprehensive chapter takes a deep dive into their involvement as significant risk factors for […]." and "A comprehensive grasp of the intricate interplay between […] and […] is pivotal for effective therapeutic strategies."
- Limits: "Our analysis is performed on the corpus level and cannot identify individual abstracts". Careful authors escape the count: "native speakers may be better at noticing and actively removing unnatural style words from LLM outputs."

Juzek and Ward (JW, abstracts only). Their method "yields 21 focal words whose increased occurrence in scientific abstracts is likely the result of LLM usage." On the cause: "We fail to find evidence that lexical overrepresentation is caused by model architecture, algorithm choices, or training data." The follow-up ties it to human feedback during training, "demonstrating that participants systematically prefer text variants that include certain words."

Reinhart et al. (RH, abstract only). Compared human and LLM texts on Biber's grammatical and rhetorical features. "These differences persist when moving from smaller models to larger ones, and are larger for instruction-tuned models than base models." WP cites this paper for present-participle clauses and for people using more qualifiers and intensifiers.

Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer (RU, abstract only). People who use LLMs a lot for writing spot machine text well: "the majority vote among five such 'expert' annotators misclassifies only 1 of 300 articles, significantly outperforming most commercial and open-source detectors we evaluated even in the presence of evasion tactics like paraphrasing and humanization." They "rely heavily on specific lexical clues ('AI vocabulary')" but "also pick up on more complex phenomena within the text (e.g., formality, originality, clarity)".

Graphite, "AI Tells" (GR). The largest current data set found. 10,000 web articles from before ChatGPT, each summarised and regenerated by nine models (eleven after the updates), then compared word by word. Graphite is a marketing agency; the data is offered for download. A tell is a word, phrase or gapped frame used at least twice as often as in the human articles.

### 4.4 Vendor documents

Anthropic (AN). Three passages in which the vendor describes its own models' writing habits and gives wording against them.

From "Prompting Claude Fable 5.1", section "Writing density": the model's "prose is denser than Claude Fable 5's: sentences run longer and there are fewer paragraph breaks. An instruction that defines the anti-pattern, mannered prose, helps." The instruction it offers:

> Mannered prose substitutes metaphor and flourish for direct statement. Instead of "a parameter worth varying," the mannered writer produces "a dial worth turning." Instead of "this point still matters," they write "this point earns its keep." The phrases exist to display the writer, not to convey the idea, and readers can tell. That is why mannered prose irritates: it makes the reader work harder so the writer can perform. It is also imprecise. Metaphors drag in connotations the writer did not choose and cannot control. The fix is to say what you mean. When a literal phrase is available, use it.

Same page, "Formatting in chat": "Earlier models overused bullets and bold in chat, and many prompts carry anti-formatting rules written to reduce that. Claude Fable 5.1 tends toward the opposite: it uses bold less and is less likely to reach for headers, lists, or quotation marks. If your prompt contains anti-formatting language, remove it or replace it with a rule that says when specific formatting is appropriate".

From "Prompting Claude Fable 5":

- "when un-steered, Claude Fable 5 can elaborate beyond what the task needs, especially at higher effort settings: surveying options it won't pursue, explaining root causes at length, producing heavily structured PR descriptions, or writing comments that narrate what the next line does."
- "Being readable and being concise are different things, and readability matters more."
- "The way to keep output short is to be selective about what you include (drop details that don't change what the reader would do next), not to compress the writing into fragments, abbreviations, arrow chains like A → B → fails, or jargon."
- "In extended or agentic conversations (many tool calls, large working context), Claude Fable 5 can produce text that's hard to follow: dense arrow-chain shorthand, deep implementation detail, references to thinking the user never saw, or overly technical phrasing."

- On em dashes: "In our sample, they are basically gone. The problem is that GPT-6 has not replaced them with richer punctuation. Instead, periods and commas account for almost 80% of the punctuation."
- "There are almost no exclamation marks. Semicolons are rare. Dashes are almost absent."
- "GPT-6 Astra uses roughly 30% nouns, compared with around 26% in the human baseline." Its examples of stacked abstract nouns: "economic security", "comprehensive social protection", "trading entitlement".
- Reduced: "Tricolons are less common. And the familiar 'not only X, but also Y' contrast appears much less frequently."

### 4.5 Editors and practitioners

The humanizer skill (HZ). An editing procedure built on WP, with a before and after for each of 26 patterns. It sorts every pattern under one idea, the default choice: "A language model writes whatever is most likely to come next, so by default it makes the choice that fits the widest range of readers and subjects. A human writer chooses for one reader and one subject, so their choices are uneven and specific."

Its six groups, in its words:

- "Staging. The sentence signals importance instead of adding a fact, with a contrast that only adds weight or a one-line closer that repeats the point."
- "Rhythm by rule. Triads and dashes applied everywhere, whether or not the meaning asks for them."
- "Inflation. Ordinary facts dressed as pivotal or expert-backed."
- "Formatting by rule. Bold and title case applied to every item."
- "Leftovers. Chat wrappers and drafting moves that were never meant for the reader."
- "Wrong reader. A reply re-explains background the other person already has, so the decision arrives last."

It ranks structure above vocabulary: "Word habits change with every model release. The structural habits above persist, so they lead the list below." And it gives a test for strength: "A tell is stronger the less often a careful writer would make it on purpose."

Other points from HZ:

- Its vocabulary list is WP's boxed list plus "Actually", "key (adjective)", "quietly" and "gate/gated/gating (figurative; keep technical uses)".
- On hedges it agrees with WP's human signs: "Ordinary hedges such as perhaps or tends to are human habits and not tells."
- On stacked qualifiers it names a cause: "Repeated editing adds one qualifier after another until every claim sounds uncertain, usually to repair an earlier overstatement rather than to report real doubt." On arguing with no one: "usually a leftover from an earlier draft."
- On mentions of a previous version: "Mention a previous version only in change logs, release notes, migration guides, and other documents about change."
- What it tells the editor to keep: "A specific, unusual detail", "Mixed feelings and unresolved tension", "Dated, era-bound references", "A genuine aside, parenthetical, or self-correction".
- Its dash rule is a blanket ban ("The final rewrite must not contain em dashes (—) or en dashes (–) unless the writer's sample uses them"), which is the kind of rule section 6 shows producing new habits.

stop-slop (SS). Eight rules and a checklist. Useful lines: "Two items beat three." "Three consecutive sentences match length? Break one." "If it sounds like a pull-quote, rewrite it." "No inanimate objects performing human actions ('the complaint becomes a fix')." "No vague declaratives ('The reasons are structural'). Name the specific thing." It also bans "all adverbs", every passive, every em dash and every sentence starting with a Wh- word. Those blanket bans go further than any evidence above supports.

Shreya Shankar (SH). Writes and reviews technical papers. Her list of what is wrong with LLM drafts:

- "Empty 'summary' sentences that pretend to conclude a thought", for example "By following these steps, we achieve better performance."
- "Overuse of bullet points and outlines"
- "Flat sentence rhythm": "When every sentence is the same length, the writing lacks rhythm and becomes harder to follow."
- "Not the right subject"
- "Low information density": "It sounds nice but says very little."
- "Vagueness"
- "Overuse of demonstrative pronouns", for example "This creates friction in production."
- "Fluency without understanding", including coined terms: "I find that LLMs make up terms that don't exist, especially for technical content." Her example is "retrieval grounding".

She also lists patterns "People Flag as 'LLM-Like,' But Are Actually Fine": intentional repetition, signposting phrases, parallel structure, section headings that echo a structure, declarative openings and em dashes. "I've seen people overcorrect in response to LLM writing habits, cutting patterns that are actually helpful when used well." "The goal isn't to avoid sounding like a model; it's to write with clarity, intention, and control." Her bar: "the contribution is commensurate with the length."

Charlie Guo (GU).

- Red herrings: academic vocabulary, absence of typos, absence of contractions.
- Tics: em dashes; "It's Not X, It's Y"; "Snappy triads" such as "Fast, efficient, and reliable." and "Think bigger. Act bolder. Move faster."; "Unearned profundity" such as "Something shifted."; "Mid-sentence questions" such as "The solution? It's simpler than you think."; "Vapid openers and transitions" such as "As technology continues to evolve," and "In today's fast-paced world,".
- Formatting: bold with no reason, Unicode arrows and multiplication signs, and emoji bullets such as "✅ Complete quarterly report".
- Content: awkward analogies ("Every chord is a puzzle piece that finally clicks into a song.") and filler: "a paragraph has said in four sentences something that could easily have been said in one." His summary of the signature: "surface polish with nothing underneath".
- On detection: "so-called 'AI writing detectors' don't work." On defence: "my own defense against AI slop isn't to worry too much about style and structure. It's to cultivate specificity".

Hollis Robbins (RB). A test based on imagery: "if you can't see anything, if nothing springs to mind, it's probably AI." Machine examples: "It's not only efficiency that matters, but also stakeholder engagement." and "Rather than focusing on obstacles, we should embrace transformative opportunities." The same constructions from a person: "It's not that I'm cheap, it's that the restaurant had too many forks." and "Instead of apologizing, she brought donuts." Her point is that the construction is not the tell; the absence of anything concrete inside it is. "Look for the absence of concrete details."

Jill Walker Rettberg (RT), a professor at the University of Bergen, names one more content-level sign: "A genre glitch is a characteristic of LLM-assisted writing where the text suddenly switches genre, typically inserting a short promotional phrase full of sensory details into an informational text." Her clearer example is from a court filing: the defendant "boarded a long-distance Amtrak passenger train to Chicago—a route famous for its scenic views of the mountains and deserts of the American West". Her explanation is that the words just written pull in the register they usually appear with. Her verdict: "It's not wrong. It's just weird." A caution from the same post: her other example was a newspaper sentence, and the paper told her afterwards that the journalist "did not use AI to write this". So a person can write one too.

Alberto Romero (RO). Argues the well-known tells are the easy ones: "The 'tells' are not disappearing, however, merely migrating from simple vocabulary and syntactic choices to deeper structural, logical, and phenomenological layers." His nine readable signs: abstraction trap, harmless filter, Latinate bias, sensing without sensing, personified callbacks, equivocation seesaw, the treadmill effect, length over substance, the subtext vacuum.

- Latinate bias: "It prefers 'utilize' to 'use,' 'facilitate' to 'help,' and 'demonstrate' to 'show.'"
- Harmless filter: "If the words feel like they were chosen by a corporate HR department trying to avoid a lawsuit, you are likely reading AI".
- Equivocation seesaw: "While X has many benefits, it is important to note that Y encompasses several challenges."
- Subtext vacuum: "AI tends to explicitly explain jokes, explicitly state themes, and explicitly connect every logical step, leaving no room for the reader to do the work".

E. Prybylski (PR), a working editor's list, last updated this month with the note that some tells "no longer hold true of some models like Claude's Opus 5.5". Items that bear on short text: "Three-part lists everywhere (everywhere)"; "Over-explanation of things to try and give context unnecessarily"; "Metaphors that sound weighty but don't actually mean anything when you think about them"; "Generic specificity (details that sound precise but aren't verifiable or things most people would notice or could notice)"; "Consistently smooth transitions rather than conversational or even jagged ones"; "Excessive reassuring of the reader, often assuming that the text knows what the reader is feeling"; "Strange aphorisms that imply some kind of wisdom without saying anything of depth or meaning 'growth happens at the edge of comfort'". On words against patterns: "While there are certainly individual words and phrases I will identify as potentially AI keywords, those change. The vibes often do not".

Sabina Varga (VA), a content writer, against banned-word lists handed to writers. Her reasons: "The lists only get longer. Where does it end?"; "Models continue to train on our writing and update their usage. Lists soon become obsolete."; "You're only addressing a symptom, not the root issue: a lack of clarity." She also reports the over-correction in people: "People are now intentionally making spelling or grammar errors to show they're human." What she recommends in place of a list: "Focus on meaning before vocabulary." "Read out loud. See where you stumble." "Avoid jargon. When you need to use it, briefly explain it." "Favor concrete over abstract language. Paint pictures people can see." "Mix and match for rhythm and texture: vary sentence length and word types."

"How to tell if AI wrote it" (UG). Four kinds of failure: lexical ("delve, leverage, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, tapestry, realm, navigate, unlock, empower"), structural, tonal ("manufactured enthusiasm, hedging stacks ('may potentially'), the customer-service apology spiral") and genre ("copy that ignores the conventions of the medium it claims to serve"). Its before and after: "In today's fast-paced media landscape, our cutting-edge AI-powered solution seamlessly empowers newsrooms to unlock unprecedented efficiencies." became "Three regional dailies now use it for pre-publication checks. None of them call the change transformative. All three call it removing a class of small, repetitive edits from the subbing pass."

Utsubo (UT), a design agency's list of signs that a website was generated. Its own prose has several of the tells it lists, so take it as a list, not a model. The copy items:

- Hero lines that fit anyone: "'Build the future of work.' 'Your all-in-one platform.' 'Scale without limits.' AI ships hero copy that could belong to a CRM, a yoga studio, or a logistics startup with equal ease".
- "Buzzword Soup": "'Seamless.' 'Robust.' 'Cutting-edge.' 'Future-ready.' 'Best-in-class.' 'Unlock value.' 'Elevate your [noun].'" And: "A page can be full of these words and still leave a reader unable to say what the product does."
- Hedged, cover-everything feature lists: "'May help you.' 'Can potentially streamline.' Feature lists led by emoji (🚀 ✨ 💡), every conceivable benefit hit, no stance taken."
- Its check for a headline: "Your hero headline names what you specifically do—no one else could use it".

### 4.6 Documentation, READMEs and change descriptions

Fabrizio Ferri-Benedetti (PU), a technical writer, on LLM-written docs: they read "like a hastily composed README file from a forsaken repo on GitHub. This READMEse dialect is an uncanny frankentongue made of cliches and bad practices that have plagued READMEs for decades. It goes against most style guides, with Latinisms, title casing, self-referentiality, overly informal style, passive voice, and other issues." On rules as a fix: "enforcing a strict style on LLM-generated text is incredibly difficult." On what is missing: "Deciding what not to include is as important as deciding what to document."

Tian Pan (TN) on agent-written pull request descriptions: "Every PR has the same four sections, the same paragraph rhythm, the same checkbox list, the same 'Test plan' header followed by three plausible bullets." The result: "The agent-generated description fills the format and skips the function." And on the fix that does not work: "A more elaborate uniform description is still a uniform description." (The studies this post cites were not traced.)

WP's edit-summary section (4.1) is the best evidence on one-line change notes. AN (4.4) adds, from the vendor, "heavily structured PR descriptions" and "comments that narrate what the next line does".

Search summaries only, pages not fetched, so no quotes: several practitioner guides list as signs of machine-written repositories a README that follows a stock outline with every section filled, comments that restate the code in a teaching voice, a doc comment on every function, and uniform comment style across a whole codebase. For shop listings, merchants' forums and vendor blogs name openers of the "Elevate your…" kind and the words versatile, premium, curated, effortless and timeless. For release notes, guides trace vague output ("improved performance") to thin input and recommend measurable statements. I found no study of machine-written interface labels, tooltips or game text. That gap is real.

### 4.7 Sources on what comes after the well-known tells

Werner Robitza (SL) describes three levels of "Claudish".

- Level 1, the familiar shapes: "'The one thing you need to X.'", "'It's not X, it's Y.' (Alternative: 'It's a real X, not Y.')", "'Honest caveat: X.'"
- Level 2: "highly compressed half-sentences, with nouns as adjectives, tech lingo and no discernible flow." "It seems that the more Claude talks with itself — through reasoning or extended tool calls — the more likely it is to produce this kind of text." His examples: "Cap the study-lifecycle handlers so a hung study can't wedge the deep-link…" and "This is a real fleet-storm collapse. When most of the fleet degrades together across many subjects in one cycle, emit one 'fleet-wide / likely measurement-side' incident instead of N."
- Level 3, writing a revision as a difference from the last version: titles that "described the delta, not the conclusion"; findings marked "Revised / Retracted / Replaced" ("Those words are about the document's history, not about the current state of things."); a "What Changed in v3" table up front; and a body written "as a refutation of what was never explained". He calls it "the giveaway tone of an AI proving its reasoning rather than informing a reader", and offers a cause: "Prose is not code, and my assumption is that the reinforcement learning of these models focuses on code edits primarily, where that diff-based approach still works."

The same author's "Writing Simply in the Age of AI" (SW) is his rewrite advice. On emphasis: "State the point plainly. Let a finding stand on its own. Don't announce that it matters with a label." On repetition: "Make each point once." On process: "the reader primarily wants what you found and what it means, not necessarily the process you used to find it." His word pairs, plain word first: "'has' / 'shows' before 'exhibits', 'reveals', 'demonstrates'"; "'is' before 'serves as', 'represents', 'sits at'"; "'use' before 'utilize', 'leverage'"; "'so' or 'because' before 'thereby' or 'in order to'". He keeps the punctuation others ban: "A colon, a semicolon, or an em-dash (yes, you can still use them! I have used them since 2015!) can hold two thoughts together."

University of Waterloo (UW), Professor Yuntian Deng: "It often relies on compact technical vocabulary and expressions like 'gated on' or 'load-bearing', words from existing technical fields, and uses them in very concise statements describing fairly complicated relationships. The result is information-dense output that's surprisingly difficult for a human reader who hasn't spent much time interacting with coding agents." It spreads to people: "I now catch myself describing things as '[something]-gated' or 'byte-identical'."

Mike Toppa (TP) quotes an agent's review comment: "…Then a permissions misconfiguration degrades to 'you don't get the 👀 and the summary' instead of 'you don't get the review.' The suggestions are the product; the acknowledgment is garnish, and garnish shouldn't be able to take the meal down." After moving to a Simplified Technical English style he reports "No weird analogies, no self-invented jargon, no long convoluted sentences, no hedging or obfuscation, and no rotating through multiple synonyms to refer to the same thing."

Pablo Oliva (OL) on an earlier model: "Opus 4.7 got terse on me." The progress reports and wrap-ups had "gotten terse. The harder the project, the harder it got to follow." His fix was a style that tells the model "to spell out the reasoning, define terms inline, stop making me connect the dots."

paddo.dev (PD) collects the complaints and Arena's figures (4.3), quotes a Hacker News comment on Opus 5 ("Sentences that orbit a point, then jump to it like it's a revealed insight. Unnecessarily abstract phraseology. Constantly using inanimate nouns as the subjects in sentences in order to unlock variety in verb choice, especially when it helps construct a sentence where the real action can 'land' like a surprise at the end."), and makes the point about rules ageing: "the rule you wrote to stop Opus 4.8 bolding everything is now the reason 5.1 will not give you a list."

mcp.directory (MD), a vendor blog built on a Reddit thread it quotes (thread not fetched). The thread's complaint, as quoted there: "Claude, maybe in an effort to be concise, creates the most dense sentences and made-up compound words that are so tedious and challenging to read. It invents concepts without defining them and bakes them into specs and plans." Terms named: "load-bearing", "blast radius", "fan out", "clique" for group, "spine", "seams", "gate", "substrate", and acronyms invented on the spot ("Claude decided to start writing 'IV' everywhere"). The blog's own account of what failed:

> First, we assumed a banned-word list alone would fix it. It didn't — Claude just cycled to synonyms the list didn't cover. "Blast radius" banned, "impact surface" appears instead. A list only works paired with the definition requirement

> Second, an early version of our own Output Style told Claude only to "write simply." It complied by over-explaining basic concepts — defining "API" mid-sentence in a post aimed at developers — which is its own kind of unreadable.

Its dividing line for jargon: "It is whether the reader can be expected to already know it."

Flavio Copes (FC) on why a vague rule does nothing: "'Concise' has no length. The model reads it, agrees, and writes the same amount." "A rule like 'list the changed file paths, nothing else' is verifiable. 'Be brief' is not."

Kuber Mehta (KM) on the cost of style rules applied while the work is done: "you are asking it to continuously compress its output into a lower-bandwidth format. That compression is lossy." And smoothing hides problems: human-sounding prose turns conflicting evidence into "There are a few considerations here."

Three short pieces on punctuation show the over-correction in people as well as in models.

- Lia Erisson (MG), a student, after detectors came into use: "At this point, I began to change my writing style. No more back-to-back 20+ word sentences. No more dash-filled phrases, semicolons, or groups of threes; I was not willing to risk being flagged." Her conclusion: "To write 'humanly', we have to write less creatively."
- Sam Woolfe (WO), a freelance writer: "A client recently gave me some feedback on an article I wrote, and they picked up on my use of en dashes", and "thought it best to avoid them, as these dashes are now associated with AI-generated content." He accepts the reason for client work: "If there's any hint that AI was used to generate the content being read, the reader can instantly lose trust."
- Abhishek Shukla (SK), May 2025, on the older habit: ChatGPT "overuses em dashes and undervalues semicolons". This is the baseline the newer models moved away from.

Hacker News (HN), two threads, keyword search only. Representative comments: "Claude seems to choose the most clever word choice it possibly can instead of the simplest. It's like the perfect inversion of how writing should be. Why say 'load-bearing' when you mean 'important'?" and "I did a grep for load-bearing in our codebase and it now appears hundreds of times."

## 6. Second-order tells

These are the signs that appear once the well-known tells have been pushed down, whether by the vendor in training, by a system prompt, or by a rule in a style guide. They are kept apart from section 5 because a guide that only bans the first-order tells is likely to produce them.

### 6.1 How much evidence there is

- Well evidenced: compression, borrowed shorthand, coined labels, and metaphor in place of plain statement. The vendor describes all four in its own models (AN), and independent writers report them (SL, UW, TP, OL, MD, HN).
- Measured: the fall in em dashes and the narrowing of punctuation (GR, IL, AR), sentence lengths bunching in one band (GR, IL), and more qualification and corrective framing in the GPT family (GR, WP).
- Reported directly as the result of a rule: a handful of cases only. A banned word replaced by its neighbour, and "write simply" turning into over-explanation (MD). A double hyphen standing in for the dash, and a list rewritten as "The first... The second..." paragraphs (TF). The em dash ban emptying out the rest of the punctuation (WP, where the sentence is marked "citation needed"). Rules against bold ending in no formatting at all (AN, PD).
- Not found: a controlled comparison of the same prompt with and without an avoid-list. Most of what follows is a change from one model version to the next, with the cause inferred.
- Little or nothing: archaic wording, unusual word order, and dropped articles as an unprompted habit (6.4).

### 6.4 Looked for, found little

- Archaic or old-fashioned wording. No source names it. The nearest findings are about formality: RO's "Latinate bias", WP's stiff synonyms ("authored", "utilized"), GR's "Across Gemini versions, contractions nearly disappear" and PG's "Rarely uses contractions". (From memory, unverified: connectives such as "whilst", "thus", "hence" and "so as to" are the kind of wording I would expect here. I found no source that says so.)
- Unusual word order. No source describes inversion or fronting as a tell. What the sources do describe is where the point is put: sentences built so that "the real action can 'land' like a surprise at the end" (a reader quoted by PD), the short tail after a comma (TF, HZ), and "colon-then-reveal sentences" (AN).
- Dropped articles. Found as a style people ask for. FC describes a popular add-on for coding agents: "It makes the agent drop articles, filler, and full sentences in the prose around the code", which gives answers like "New object ref each render. Inline object prop = new ref = re-render. Wrap in `useMemo`." An agent run with such a setting may carry the habit into commit messages and notes. For unprompted output the sources speak of "fragments" (AN) and missing subjects ("No configuration file needed.", HZ). None of them counts articles.
- Semicolon and colon overuse. Mixed, as 6.3 shows.
- Short product text. None of these sources tested labels, tooltips or game text. The last column of each table is my reading.

### 6.5 The same over-correction in people

Writers who fear being taken for a machine narrow their writing in the same way. MG gave up long sentences, dashes, semicolons and threes together. WO was asked by a client to drop dashes. VA reports people "intentionally making spelling or grammar errors to show they're human." The habits also pass from the tools to their users: "I now catch myself describing things as '[something]-gated' or 'byte-identical'." (UW) WP makes the general point: "human speech and writing is being influenced by LLMs".

### 6.6 What the cases have in common

This is my reading of the evidence above.

- Each replacement is the nearest neighbour of what was removed. A banned word gives way to its synonym, the dash to a semicolon or a double hyphen, the bulleted list to "The first... The second...".
- The rules that produced them say what to take out and nothing about what to put in. Or they push on one measure (shorter, fewer words, no dashes) and leave the rest to drift.
- The rules that sources report as working name what the reader needs and settle the trade-off in advance. AN's wording is the clearest case: "Write complete sentences. Spell out terms." and "If you have to choose between short and clear, choose clear."

### 7.2 Rules

Words

- Short and common: "Use 'buy' instead of 'purchase', 'help' instead of 'assist', and 'about' instead of 'approximately'." (GOV)
- GOV's "Words to avoid" overlaps the machine vocabulary lists: collaborate, deliver, empower, facilitate, foster, key, leverage, robust, streamline, tackle, transform, utilise, and "in order to, usually not needed".
- No metaphors: "Avoid using metaphors - they do not say what you actually mean and lead to slower comprehension of your content." (GOV) Mailchimp avoids "fluffy metaphors and cheap plays to emotion".
- Specialist terms are allowed if explained: "You just need to explain what they mean the first time you use them." (GOV) The other side of the same rule: "don't define something that's obvious to the user." (PL)
- The reader's own words: "Use the language that users" are "familiar with" and "use themselves". (GOV)
- One name for one thing: "Avoid using synonyms for the same word in a single piece of writing." (MC)
- Verbs, not nouns made from verbs: "We analyze data." in place of "We conduct an analysis of the data." (PL)
- No noun strings (PL). "For example", not "eg" (GOVUI, PL). No slashes: "Apart from fractions, the slash has almost no good uses." (PL)
- "Avoid unnecessary modifiers." "Avoid vague language." "Use specific examples." (MC)

Sentences

- Active voice (GOV, PL, MC, GD). GOV allows the passive "when the outcome is more important than the agent of the action".
- Present tense: "The simplest and strongest form of a verb is present tense." (PL)
- Positive form: avoid double negatives and "exceptions to exceptions" (PL). "Use positive language rather than negative language." (MC)
- Address the reader as "you" (GOV, GD).
- Contractions: "Use contractions like it's, you'll, you're, we're, and let's." (MS) GOV and GOVUI accept "you'll" and avoid negative ones such as "can't" and "don't", which some readers misread.
- "Most of the time, start each statement with a verb. Edit out you can when it isn't necessary." (MS)
- "Put conditions before instructions, not after." (GD)
- Vary the length: "Like sentence length, if all paragraphs are the same size your writing will be choppy." and "There is nothing wrong with an occasional one-sentence paragraph." (PL)

Structure

- Most important first (GOV, MS, MC, NN: "the inverted pyramid style, starting with the conclusion").
- Headings that describe: "avoid generic headings like 'Introduction'". Sentence case (GOV, GOVUI, MS, GD).
- "Don't pre-announce anything in documentation." (GD) "Do not repeat the summary in the first paragraph." (GOV)
- Numbered lists for steps and bullets for other lists (GD, MC).
- Bold for interface elements only: "Do not use bold in other situations, for example to emphasise text." (GOV) "Put UI elements in bold." (GD)
- "Don't use a period or a colon at the end of titles, headings, subheadings, and UI titles." (MS)

Interface text

- "So start with less." Add help text only "if user research shows that you need it". (GOVUI)
- "If you find yourself having to explain how the user interface works, that's a sign something has gone wrong." (GOVUI)
- Drop the words that carry nothing: "there's usually no need to say 'This is the total cost'. Just say 'Total cost'." And "do not say 'You have entered the wrong password'. Say 'Wrong password'." (GOVUI)
- "You do not usually need the word 'now'." (GOVUI)
- "Headings and input field labels are sentence case, but not punctuated. Write other copy in full sentences, with a full stop at the end." (GOVUI)
- Buttons: "Button copy should always include verbs." (MC)
- Tooltips: "Tooltips with obvious or redundant text are not beneficial to users. If you can't think of particularly helpful content, don't offer a tooltip." Nothing needed for the task belongs in one. (NN)
- Error messages: say exactly what happened ("Generic messages such as An error occurred lack context."), offer a remedy, and do not blame: avoid "invalid, illegal, or incorrect". "Avoid humor since it can become stale if users encounter the error frequently." (NN) Microsoft's example: "Invalid ID" becomes "You need an ID that looks like this: someone@example.com".
- Do not point by colour or position, as in "click the green button". (GOVUI)
- "Do not follow strict grammar conventions if it makes things clearer not to." (GOVUI)

Tone

- GOV's list: "specific", "informative", "clear and concise", "brisk, but not terse", "serious but not pompous", and "emotionless – adjectives can be subjective and make the text sound more emotive and like spin".
- "There's usually no need to say 'please' or 'please note'." "There's no need to say 'sorry' in validation error messages." "There's usually no need to say thank you." (GOVUI)
- "If people do not notice your copy, you're probably doing it right. Aim to be boring." (GOVUI, written for government services)
- "it's always more important to be clear than entertaining", and "forced humor can be worse than none at all." (MC)
- Facts over sales talk: "Users detested 'marketese'" (NN). "Avoid dramatic storytelling and grandiose claims." (MC)
- A check two guides share: "Read your text aloud." (MS) "When in doubt, read your writing out loud." (MC)

### 7.3 Where the guidance pulls in two directions

- Short sentences. The plain-language sources ask for short sentences; GR, IL and WP find that short sentences of even length are a machine sign. PL already holds the answer: vary the length.
- Devices for scanning. In NN's 1997 test the "scannable" version, with "bulleted lists, boldface text to highlight keywords", more headings and shorter sections, did 47% better. The same devices applied to everything are now on the tell lists (WPS, TF, HZ). GOV's narrower rule fits both: lists for real lists and steps, bold for interface elements only.
- Dropping words. GOVUI wants "Wrong password" and "Total cost". HZ treats "No configuration file needed." as a tell. The line GOVUI draws works for both: labels and messages may be fragments, and "other copy" is written "in full sentences".
- Short against clear. "Shorter is always better." (MS) Against that: "Write briefly, but don't sacrifice clarity for brevity." (MC), "brisk, but not terse" (GOV), and "If you have to choose between short and clear, choose clear." (AN)
- "There is" and "in order to". MS says to avoid "weak phrasing like there is, there are, and there were", and GOV and PL cut "in order to". WP lists both among the signs of human writing. Plain and human-sounding are two different aims, and they usually agree but not here.
- Question headings. GOV: headings should not "be questions". GOVUI: "It's fine to have headings as questions."
- Capitals on buttons. Mailchimp's Web Elements page says buttons "use sentence case"; its summary page says "Capitalize every word, including articles."
- Contractions. MS and MC use them freely. GOV avoids the negative ones for readability. In GR's data "the human articles use contractions and the second person much more often" than the generated ones, so a text with no contractions at all leans towards the machine side.

## 8. What does not work, and what does

Almost all of this is practitioner report and vendor advice. Only the detector and reader studies (RU, and those WP cites) and NN's 1997 test are experiments.

### 8.1 What does not work

- Detectors as proof. WP: "these tools have non-trivial error rates." GU: "so-called 'AI writing detectors' don't work." GR: "we do not recommend classifying authorship based on individual tells."
- Judging by a general feeling. WP: "Humans are notoriously bad at distinguishing human and LLM-generated text." WP also lists "bland" or "robotic" prose, perfect grammar and formal wording in general as ineffective signs. The exception matters for a public site: people who use these tools a lot are very good at it. In RU, five such readers together misjudged "only 1 of 300 articles".
- "Humanizing" the text afterwards with a tool. RU's experienced readers stayed accurate "even in the presence of evasion tactics like paraphrasing and humanization."
- A banned-word list alone. The model moves to the next word (MD). Lists age: GR's "55% to 72%" turnover between versions; VA's "Lists soon become obsolete."; HZ's "Word habits change with every model release." WP adds that a word's synonyms are not suspect just because the word is.
- Vague instructions. "'Concise' has no length. The model reads it, agrees, and writes the same amount." (FC) "Write simply" produced over-explanation (MD). "Being readable and being concise are different things" (AN).
- Blanket bans on ordinary tools. SS bans every adverb, every passive and every em dash; HZ bans dashes unless a sample has them. The recorded result of the dash ban in models is flatter punctuation that "has become its own sign of AI writing" (WP). SH: "I've seen people overcorrect in response to LLM writing habits, cutting patterns that are actually helpful when used well."
- Rules written against one model's habits. "the rule you wrote to stop Opus 4.8 bolding everything is now the reason 5.1 will not give you a list." (PD) AN tells prompt writers to take such rules out.
- Treating the signs as the fault. WP: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder." VA: "You're only addressing a symptom, not the root issue: a lack of clarity." SH: "The goal isn't to avoid sounding like a model; it's to write with clarity, intention, and control."
- Smoothing as a goal. KM: human-sounding prose can turn conflicting evidence into "There are a few considerations here." The text reads well and says less.
- A fuller template. "A more elaborate uniform description is still a uniform description." (TN)
- Style rules alone on generated text. "enforcing a strict style on LLM-generated text is incredibly difficult." (PU)
- Patching phrase by phrase. HZ tells the editor to "State each point naturally instead of patching flagged phrases one at a time."

### 8.2 What sources report as working

- Say what to write, and show it. "Positive examples of the communication style you want tend to be more effective than instructions about what not to do." (AN)
- Where something is to be avoided, define it and give the reason. "An instruction that defines the anti-pattern, mannered prose, helps." (AN) On word lists: "A list only works paired with the definition requirement" (MD).
- Rules that can be checked. "A rule like 'list the changed file paths, nothing else' is verifiable. 'Be brief' is not." (FC)
- Say when a device is right, in place of banning it: replace anti-formatting language "with a rule that says when specific formatting is appropriate" (AN).
- Shorten by leaving things out. "be selective about what you include (drop details that don't change what the reader would do next)" (AN). "Deciding what not to include is as important as deciding what to document." (PU) SH's bar: "the contribution is commensurate with the length."
- Be specific. WP's account of the cause is that the specific fact is replaced by the generic one, so the repair is the specific fact. GU: "cultivate specificity". RB: "Look for the absence of concrete details." UT's check for a headline: it "names what you specifically do—no one else could use it". HZ: "Where the text is generic, add a specific detail you know to be true. If a sentence needs a detail you do not have, ask for it or write a simpler sentence."
- Write for one reader, in that reader's words. "A human writer chooses for one reader and one subject" (HZ). "The vocabulary you built up while working is yours, not theirs" (AN). The test for a term: "whether the reader can be expected to already know it." (MD)
- Define a term where it first appears and keep one name for one thing (GOV, MC, OL). TP's result with a restricted, simplified English: "no self-invented jargon, no long convoluted sentences, no hedging or obfuscation, and no rotating through multiple synonyms to refer to the same thing."
- Use the plain verb: "is" and "has" before "serves as" and "features" (WP's signs of human writing, SW, HZ).
- State the point once and stop. "Don't announce that it matters with a label." "Make each point once." (SW) Cut the closing line that repeats it (HZ).
- Describe the thing as it is. Keep how it changed for change logs and release notes (HZ), and in those, say what was added in plain terms, as people do: "Added info about the artist's debut" (WP's example of a human edit summary).
- Read it aloud (MC, MS, VA, HZ).
- Work from a sample of the intended voice. HZ: "If the user gives a writing sample, read it first and match its sentence length, word choice, punctuation, openings, and transitions."
- A separate pass after drafting: mark the tells, rewrite, check that no fact was lost, then look again for the ones that most often survive, which HZ names as "contrasts", "closers", "triads", "dashes" and "bold labels". KM's point about lossy style rules during the work supports doing this afterwards; that inference is mine.
- Judge by clusters. "any of these patterns used once might be fine. The problem is when multiple tropes appear together or when a single trope is used repeatedly." (TF) And weigh each one: "A tell is stronger the less often a careful writer would make it on purpose." (HZ)
- Keep what people do. GR's markers of the human articles are first person, direct address, casual qualifiers such as "pretty" and "sort of", exclamation marks and asides. HZ tells the editor to keep "A specific, unusual detail" and "A genuine aside, parenthetical, or self-correction". VA: "vary sentence length and word types."
- Prefer facts to sales talk. This one has a measurement: objective wording in place of promotional scored 27% better in NN's test.

What to check first in each kind of text:

- Button or label. Is it the word a player would use, in sentence case, with no decoration? Is the same thing called the same name everywhere?
- Tooltip or hint. Does it say something the label does not? NN: "If you can't think of particularly helpful content, don't offer a tooltip."
- Error or empty state. Does it say what happened and what to do, without apology, blame or a joke that will wear thin?
- How-to-play paragraph. Does it open with what the player does? Is there an opening or closing sentence that could be cut with nothing lost? Are the sentences of different lengths?
- One-line blurb. Could the line describe another game? If it could, it needs a fact that belongs to this one. Look for a pair of adjectives, a three, or a "not just".
- Release note. Does it say what changed for the player, in the player's words? Look for "improved", "enhanced", internal names, and mention of what was kept.
- Help page. Is there a heading, list or bold label that the content does not need? Does any sentence explain what the screen already shows?
